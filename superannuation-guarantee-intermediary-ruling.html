Who Pays Super for Agency and Contractor Staff? SGR 2026/D1 Explained

Quick answer

When a worker is engaged through a staffing agency, locum firm, recruitment company or service entity rather than directly, the business that manages them day to day is not always the one legally responsible for their superannuation guarantee. The ATO's draft ruling SGR 2026/D1, Superannuation Guarantee: work arranged by intermediaries, released 17 June 2026, sets out how to work out who "the employer" actually is in these three-way arrangements. The test turns on who holds ultimate legal control over the worker under the contract, not who directs their daily tasks, and getting it wrong exposes the true employer to the Superannuation Guarantee Charge regardless of who thought they'd already paid it.

Medical practices, allied health clinics and NDIS providers all lean heavily on staffing arrangements that sit outside a simple direct-employment relationship: locum doctors booked through an agency, allied health practitioners engaged via a service entity or practice trust, and support workers supplied through a labour hire firm. The ATO's newest draft ruling goes directly at the question these arrangements keep raising: when three parties are involved, who is actually on the hook for superannuation guarantee?

What SGR 2026/D1 Actually Covers

On 17 June 2026, the Australian Taxation Office released draft ruling SGR 2026/D1, Superannuation Guarantee: work arranged by intermediaries, with the consultation period closing 31 July 2026. It addresses tripartite working arrangements, meaning three parties instead of two: an end-user that needs the work done, an intermediary that supplies the worker (commonly a labour hire firm, recruitment agency or service firm), and the worker themselves. Once finalised, it will replace the ATO's existing guidance in SGR 2005/2, which has stood as the primary word on this exact question for over two decades.

The core problem SGR 2026/D1 tries to resolve is that in a two-party relationship, working out who the employer is for superannuation guarantee purposes is usually straightforward. Add a third party, an agency or service entity sitting between the end-user and the worker, and it stops being obvious which side of that arrangement actually carries the superannuation guarantee obligation, particularly where the worker is paid by the intermediary but directed day to day by the end-user.

Why "Who's the Employer" Isn't Always Obvious

Intermediary arrangements are common across all three of our client verticals for good reason: they offer flexibility a direct hire doesn't. A medical centre books a locum GP through a locum agency for a run of shifts. An allied health clinic contracts a physiotherapist through a service entity that also handles the clinic's billing and room hire. An NDIS provider fills a roster gap with a support worker supplied by a staffing agency. In each case, up to three different businesses could plausibly be described as involved in employing that worker, and only one of them is actually liable for superannuation guarantee if it turns out to be owed.

Historically, businesses in this position have often assumed that because the worker holds an ABN, or because another party in the chain is paying an invoice that includes a super component, the obligation has been dealt with somewhere along the line. SGR 2026/D1 is explicit that this assumption isn't a substitute for actually identifying the legally liable party under the contract.

The Legal Test: Contract, Labour and Control

SGR 2026/D1 works from the same statutory base as every other superannuation guarantee question: section 12 of the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992. The analysis starts by identifying whether a contract for the performance of work exists, and between which of the three parties, then asks whether an employment relationship arises under either the ordinary common law meaning of employee or the extended meaning in section 12(3), which deems a person to be an employee of another if they work under a contract that is wholly or principally for their labour.

That extended definition is not new. It was tested directly in Dental Corporation Pty Ltd v Moffet [2020] FCAFC 118, where the Full Federal Court found a dentist providing services through a service entity structure was still an employee for superannuation guarantee purposes, because the substance of the arrangement was a contract for his labour, regardless of how the paperwork was structured. Dental Corp's subsequent application to the High Court for special leave to appeal was refused. The ATO's earlier ruling TR 2023/4 sets out the ordinary and extended meaning of employee for section 12 generally, and SGR 2026/D1 applies that same framework to the specific question of which party in a three-way arrangement is the one actually bound by the contract for labour. What SGR 2026/D1 adds is a clear statement that in these tripartite arrangements, it is ultimate or legal control over the worker under the contract that identifies the employer, not which party manages the worker's day-to-day tasks.

Why This Matters for Medical, Allied Health and NDIS Providers

These three verticals rely on intermediary staffing more than most industries, which is exactly why this draft ruling is worth reading now rather than waiting for it to be finalised. A medical practice using a service trust to pay contractor GPs or specialists is a textbook tripartite arrangement. An allied health clinic that engages practitioners through a related management entity, rather than employing them directly, sits in the same position. An NDIS provider topping up its roster through a labour hire agency needs to know whether it or the agency is the one that carries the superannuation guarantee liability if a support worker is later found to have been misclassified.

None of this replaces the separate question of state payroll tax on contractor payments, which our companion article on the medical practice payroll tax ruling covers in detail. Superannuation guarantee and payroll tax are assessed under entirely different legislation, by different revenue authorities, and it is entirely possible to be liable for one, both, or neither, depending on how a specific arrangement is structured.

How It Connects to Payday Super From 1 July 2026

SGR 2026/D1 arrives at a pointed moment. From 1 July 2026, the Payday Super reforms require employers to pay superannuation guarantee within 7 business days of each payday, based on an employee's qualifying earnings, rather than the previous quarterly cycle. Under the old system, a business that had misidentified the correct employer in a tripartite arrangement had up to a quarter to notice and correct the error before a payment was actually missed. Under Payday Super, that same misclassification produces a missed payment window on every single pay cycle, weekly or fortnightly for most staffing arrangements, rather than once every three months.

In practice, that means the cost of getting the SGR 2026/D1 analysis wrong has gone up substantially, not because the underlying law has changed, but because the frequency at which an error compounds has increased from four times a year to as often as fifty-two.

What Happens If You Get It Wrong

If the party legally responsible for superannuation guarantee under SGR 2026/D1's framework hasn't paid it, that party is exposed to the Superannuation Guarantee Charge, regardless of whether another business in the arrangement believed the obligation had already been met somewhere else in the chain. The charge is not tax deductible, includes interest calculated from the date the payment was originally due, and carries an administration fee assessed per employee per period. Because Payday Super shortens the cycle these errors compound on, a misidentified employer relationship discovered late can produce a liability across many more payment periods than it would have under the old quarterly system.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is SGR 2026/D1?

SGR 2026/D1, Superannuation Guarantee: work arranged by intermediaries, is a draft ruling released by the Australian Taxation Office on 17 June 2026, with the consultation period closing 31 July 2026. It explains how to identify who is legally the employer for superannuation guarantee purposes when a worker is engaged through a three-way arrangement involving an end-user, an intermediary such as a labour hire firm, recruitment agency or service entity, and the worker. Once finalised it will replace the ATO's existing guidance in SGR 2005/2.

Who is responsible for paying super when a worker is engaged through an agency?

It depends on who holds ultimate legal control over the worker under the contractual arrangement, not on which business manages the worker day to day or which business the worker invoices. SGR 2026/D1 sets out that this is assessed by first identifying whether a contract for the performance of work exists and between which parties, then working out whether an employment relationship arises under the ordinary or extended meaning of employee in section 12 of the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992.

Does using a service entity or practice trust avoid superannuation guarantee obligations?

Not automatically. Section 12(3) of the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992 deems a person to be an employee of another if they work under a contract that is wholly or principally for their labour, regardless of what the contract calls them or whether they hold an ABN. The Full Federal Court's decision in Dental Corporation Pty Ltd v Moffet [2020] FCAFC 118 confirmed this extended definition applies even where a practice uses a service entity structure, and the ATO has since confirmed in TR 2023/4 that the substance of the working relationship is what matters, not its label.

How does SGR 2026/D1 affect payday super?

From 1 July 2026, employers must pay superannuation guarantee within 7 business days of each payday rather than quarterly under the Payday Super reforms. If a business has misidentified who is legally the employer in an intermediary arrangement, that error now compounds on every single pay run instead of every quarter, so getting the SGR 2026/D1 analysis right before 1 July 2026 matters more than it did under the old quarterly cycle.

What happens if the wrong party pays superannuation guarantee?

If the party legally responsible for superannuation guarantee has not paid it, even where another party in the arrangement believed they had covered it, the liable employer is exposed to the Superannuation Guarantee Charge. This charge is not tax deductible, includes interest calculated from when the payment was originally due, and carries an administration fee per employee per period, assessed by the Australian Taxation Office regardless of which party in the arrangement made the payment error.
