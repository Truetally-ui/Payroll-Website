If I asked you to define payroll compliance in one sentence, could you actually do it? Most business owners can't, not because they're careless, but because "payroll compliance" gets talked about like it's one thing, one box to tick, when it's actually three completely separate legal obligations running at the same time. So today I want to break down what payroll compliance actually means in Australia, who's checking each part of it, and what happens when one of the three slips without anyone noticing.

[SECTION 1 — WHAT PAYROLL COMPLIANCE ACTUALLY MEANS]
At its core, payroll compliance means every employee is paid what they're legally owed, on time, with the right superannuation going into their fund, and with that pay event reported accurately to the ATO. Sounds simple enough. But here's the catch, each of those pieces sits under different legislation and gets checked by a different body. You can have your award rates completely correct and still be non-compliant because super's running late. You can have super perfectly on time and still be exposed because your Single Touch Payroll reporting is mapped wrong. That's why a general feeling that "payroll's fine" really doesn't mean much on its own, each piece needs to be checked on its own terms.

[SECTION 2 — THE THREE OBLIGATIONS]
Let's go through the three. First, award or agreement pay, that's the base rate, penalty rates, overtime and allowances, all matched to what someone's actually doing in their role, not just their job title, and that sits under the Fair Work Act 2009. Second, superannuation guarantee, currently 12 percent of ordinary time earnings, paid into each employee's fund, and from the first of July 2026 that shifts to Payday Super, meaning it's due in line with every pay run instead of quarterly. That one sits under the Superannuation Guarantee Administration Act. Third, Single Touch Payroll reporting, which under Phase 2 requires a lot more detail with every single pay event than it used to, disaggregated pay components, an income type, a cessation reason when someone leaves. On top of those three, you've also got payslips, which need to go out within one working day under the Fair Work Regulations, and records, which need to be kept for at least seven years. Those last two don't create an obligation on their own, but they're what you lean on to prove the other three were actually met.

[SECTION 3 — WHO'S ACTUALLY ENFORCING THIS]
Here's something that trips a lot of businesses up, there's no single regulator watching all of this at once. The Fair Work Ombudsman enforces award rates and the National Employment Standards under the Fair Work Act. The ATO handles superannuation guarantee and Single Touch Payroll separately from that. And on top of both of those, your state or territory revenue office enforces payroll tax, which is yet another obligation again, based on total wages paid above a threshold rather than individual entitlements. So you can be completely compliant with one of these regulators and still be exposed under another, which is exactly why a proper payroll review checks each obligation against its own rules rather than just glancing at a clean payroll software report.

[SECTION 4 — WHAT HAPPENS WHEN IT GOES WRONG]
The consequences really depend on which piece slipped. Underpay wages, and you're looking at Fair Work Ombudsman enforcement, back payment orders, and since the first of January 2025, if the underpayment's intentional, it's now a criminal offence under the Fair Work Act, we're talking penalties of up to ten years imprisonment for an individual. Miss or delay super, and the ATO hits you with the Superannuation Guarantee Charge, which isn't tax deductible and comes with interest and an admin fee stacked on top. Get STP reporting wrong, and that can trigger its own separate ATO compliance activity, even if the underlying pay and super were actually correct. Worth saying though, an honest mistake and a deliberate underpayment are treated very differently, that criminal threshold specifically targets intentional conduct, which is exactly why having a documented, regularly reviewed process matters more now than it used to.

[SECTION 5 — HOW TO ACTUALLY STAY ON TOP OF IT]
So how do you stay compliant across all three at once? You check each one on its own terms, on a standing basis, not as a one-off setup you did when the business started. Award classifications get reassessed as duties change, not just when someone's hired. Super gets calculated on the correct ordinary time earnings figure at the current rate, with systems ready for Payday Super. STP categories stay mapped correctly as pay items change. And if you're running the SCHADS Award, which covers a lot of NDIS and allied health businesses, this needs even more frequent checking, because classification levels and penalty rates shift more often as rosters and duties change.
