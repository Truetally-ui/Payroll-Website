Payday Super First-Year Compliance: What PCG 2026/1 Means for Your Payroll

Quick answer

PCG 2026/1, finalised by the ATO on 28 January 2026, sets out how the ATO will allocate compliance resources for superannuation guarantee shortfalls occurring under Payday Super between 1 July 2026 and 30 June 2027. It sorts employer behaviour into a low, medium or high risk zone based on how quickly a shortfall is corrected after the payday it relates to. It is not a legal safe harbour, an unresolved shortfall still triggers the superannuation guarantee charge regardless of risk zone, but correcting errors fast is what keeps an employer out of the ATO's priority queue for compliance action in the transition year.

Payday Super started on 1 July 2026, and most of the coverage since has focused on setting it up, connecting a clearing house that can process super on the same cycle as wages, and mapping ordinary time earnings correctly. Less has been said about what happens after go-live, when something inevitably slips. The ATO's answer is PCG 2026/1, a Practical Compliance Guideline that spells out exactly how forgiving, or unforgiving, the first year is going to be.

What PCG 2026/1 Actually Is

PCG 2026/1 is a Practical Compliance Guideline, finalised by the ATO on 28 January 2026, covering the first year Payday Super operates. It applies to superannuation guarantee shortfalls relating to a qualifying earnings day, the ATO's term for a payday that triggers a super guarantee obligation, occurring anywhere from 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2027 inclusive. Rather than setting a new legal test, the guideline describes how the ATO will prioritise its compliance resources during that transition period, and it does that by placing employer behaviour into one of three risk zones.

It is worth being precise about what this guideline is not. It is not an exemption from the superannuation guarantee charge, and it is not a grace period in the sense of extra time before the law applies. The 7-business-day payment requirement under Payday Super still applies from 1 July 2026. What PCG 2026/1 changes is the likelihood that a given shortfall becomes the subject of an ATO review during the first twelve months, based on how the employer behaved once the shortfall occurred.

The Three Risk Zones Explained

ZoneWhat puts an employer hereATO's stated approach
Low riskThe employer genuinely intended to pay on time and made the contribution, but it was not received by the fund on time for reasons outside its control (for example, a clearing house delay), and the shortfall is corrected as soon as reasonably practicableThe ATO states it will not have cause to review the employer's actions
Medium riskThe employer does not meet the low risk criteria, but every affected employee's individual final SG shortfall is brought to nil within 28 days of the end of the quarter in which the qualifying earnings were paid (for example, an employer still paying on a quarterly rhythm rather than the new 7-day window, but ultimately squaring up)Given lower priority than high risk arrangements, but not automatically cleared
High riskAn individual employee's final SG shortfall is still greater than nil after that 28-day window has passedThe ATO's stated compliance priority for the transition year

The pattern across all three zones is the same: speed of correction is what the ATO is measuring, not whether an error happened in the first place. An employer that never makes a mistake in twelve months of weekly or fortnightly pay runs is unrealistic to expect. An employer that catches and fixes a shortfall inside 28 days of quarter-end is the baseline the guideline is built around.

Qualifying Earnings Days and the 7-Day Window

A qualifying earnings, or QE, day is the payday that triggers a superannuation guarantee obligation under Payday Super. The payment types that count as qualifying earnings are broadly the same payments that were already included in super guarantee calculations before 1 July 2026, such as ordinary time earnings. Once a QE day occurs, the baseline legal requirement is that the super contribution reaches the employee's fund within 7 business days.

PCG 2026/1's risk zones sit downstream of that 7-day rule. Missing the 7-day window on its own does not automatically place an employer in the high risk zone, what matters is what happens next: whether the shortfall is corrected as soon as reasonably practicable (low risk), within 28 days of the end of the quarter (medium risk), or left unresolved beyond that point (high risk).

Why This Matters More for NDIS, Medical and Allied Health Payroll

The risk-zone framework is written for every employer, but it lands harder on organisations with complex payroll. NDIS providers running large casual support worker rosters under the Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award, medical practices with a mix of employed and contracted practitioners, and allied health clinics paying clinicians across multiple pay cycles all have more moving parts than a single-cycle, salaried workforce. Every additional pay cycle, timesheet feed or roster import is another point where a qualifying earnings figure can be miscalculated or a contribution can miss the fund on time.

Where an organisation also engages workers through an intermediary, a labour hire arrangement or a service entity, working out who the employer is for superannuation guarantee purposes adds another layer of complexity on top of the timing requirement itself. That question is the subject of the ATO's draft ruling on intermediary arrangements, which sits alongside PCG 2026/1 as part of the same broader Payday Super compliance picture.

What Happens If a Shortfall Isn't Fixed

A superannuation guarantee shortfall that isn't corrected triggers the Superannuation Guarantee Charge under the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992, which is not tax deductible and includes an interest component calculated from the date the contribution was due, plus an administration fee. Falling into the high risk zone under PCG 2026/1 doesn't itself increase the charge, but it does move the employer to the front of the queue for the ATO's compliance activity during the first year Payday Super operates, at a time when the ATO has explicitly said it is watching how employers behave while the system is new.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is PCG 2026/1?

PCG 2026/1 is a Practical Compliance Guideline finalised by the ATO on 28 January 2026. It sets out how the ATO will allocate its compliance resources when investigating a superannuation guarantee shortfall for a qualifying earnings day occurring between 1 July 2026 and 30 June 2027, the first year Payday Super operates. It sorts employer behaviour into a low, medium or high risk zone rather than creating a legal exemption from the superannuation guarantee charge.

What are the three risk zones under PCG 2026/1?

The low risk zone covers employers who genuinely intended to pay on time but a contribution was not received by the fund on time for reasons outside their control, and who correct the shortfall as soon as reasonably practicable; the ATO says it will not have cause to review these employers' actions. The medium risk zone covers employers who do not meet the low risk criteria but still bring every employee's final shortfall to nil within 28 days of the end of the quarter in which the qualifying earnings were paid. The high risk zone covers employers whose individual final shortfalls are still greater than nil after that 28-day window, and these are the ATO's compliance priority.

What happens if you miss a Payday Super payment in the first year?

Missing the payment does not automatically trigger an audit. What matters under PCG 2026/1 is how quickly it is fixed. Correcting the shortfall as soon as reasonably practicable can keep an employer in the low risk zone, and resolving it within 28 days of the end of the relevant quarter keeps it in the medium risk zone. A shortfall left unresolved beyond that point places the employer in the high risk zone and in the ATO's priority queue for compliance action, including the superannuation guarantee charge.

Is PCG 2026/1 a legal safe harbour from the superannuation guarantee charge?

No. PCG 2026/1 describes where the ATO will focus its compliance resources during the transition year, it does not change the law. An employer with an SG shortfall still owes the superannuation guarantee charge under the Superannuation Guarantee (Administration) Act 1992 regardless of which risk zone they fall into, the risk zone only affects how likely that shortfall is to draw an ATO review in the first year.

Why does PCG 2026/1 matter more for NDIS, medical and allied health payroll?

These sectors typically run large casual and part-time workforces, multiple concurrent pay cycles, and rostering or timesheet systems feeding into payroll, all of which increase the chance a qualifying earnings calculation or clearing house payment slips outside the required window. A provider or practice with dozens of support workers or clinicians paid on different cycles has more points of failure than a business with a single fortnightly payroll, which makes the low and medium risk criteria in PCG 2026/1 directly relevant to how quickly errors need to be caught and corrected.
