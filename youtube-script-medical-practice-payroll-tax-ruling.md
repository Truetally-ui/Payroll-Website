[HOOK]
If you run a medical practice with contractor GPs, you've probably had someone mention "the new payroll tax ruling" at some point in the last couple of years, usually followed by a lot of uncertainty about what it actually means for you. Here's the thing, there isn't one single new ruling. There's a court case that reshaped how every state looks at contractor GP payments, and a rolling series of state-by-state responses since. Today I'm walking through what actually happened, how each state has responded, and what it means for your contractor arrangements right now.

[SECTION 1 — THE CASE THAT STARTED IT]
It all traces back to a case called Thomas and Naaz. This was a group of medical centres in Western Sydney that engaged GPs as contractors, a really common model, where the GP keeps around seventy percent of what they bill, and the practice keeps the rest as a service fee for the room, the reception, the admin. Revenue NSW came in and assessed payroll tax on those payments to the GPs, on the basis that even though the money technically came from patients and Medicare, it was the practice making the payment to the doctor, and that counted as wages. This went through the tribunal, then the tribunal's appeal panel, then the New South Wales Court of Appeal, and at every single stage, the finding held. The payments were wages. The practice was the deemed employer. And this built on an earlier Victorian case involving an optical business, which had already established that these relevant contract rules get read pretty broadly.

[SECTION 2 — WHY "RELEVANT CONTRACTS" IS THE KEY PHRASE]
The legal mechanism here is something called relevant contract provisions, and most state payroll tax laws have a version of this. What it does is let a payment be deemed wages even when there's no employment relationship at all, as long as it's a payment under a contract for someone's services, unless a specific exemption applies. There is an exemption for someone who provides services to the public generally as part of their own independent business, but Thomas and Naaz showed that exemption gets read narrowly. If the GP can't really refuse the patients they're allocated, if the payment flows through the practice rather than directly from patient to doctor, if there's an exclusivity clause stopping them working anywhere else, those all work against the exemption applying.

[SECTION 3 — HOW EACH STATE HAS RESPONDED]
Now here's where it gets genuinely state-specific, and this is the part that trips people up the most. After Thomas and Naaz, state revenue offices didn't all do the same thing. Queensland, New South Wales, South Australia, Victoria and the ACT have each rolled out some kind of relief, usually starting with a temporary amnesty, and in several cases now replaced by an ongoing exemption or rebate, generally tied to how much of the practice's billing is bulk billed. Western Australia, Tasmania and the Northern Territory haven't introduced an equivalent concession, so the ordinary relevant contract rules just keep applying there. And here's the catch even in the states with relief, it's usually GP wages specifically. Your nurses, your reception staff, your allied health contractors, your non-GP specialists, none of that is automatically covered by a GP-specific exemption.

[SECTION 4 — WHAT THIS ACTUALLY MEANS FOR YOUR PRACTICE]
So what do you actually do with this? First, figure out which state, or states, your practice operates in, because the answer is genuinely different in each one, and if you're multi-state, you could be exempt in one location and fully exposed in another under the exact same contract template. Second, don't assume that because your GP arrangements are sorted, the rest of your contractor base is too, because it almost certainly isn't covered by the same exemption. And third, if there was an amnesty that applied to your practice's history, check whether it actually wrote off any unpaid liability, or just paused enforcement while your file gets reviewed, because those are very different outcomes and it's worth knowing which one you're actually sitting in.

[SECTION 5 — WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT]
There's no template contract that guarantees you sit outside these rules, because every revenue office looks at how the arrangement actually operates day to day, not just what the paperwork says. But the things that keep coming up again and again are whether the practitioner can genuinely refuse patients and set their own hours, whether billing goes directly between the patient and the practitioner rather than being pooled and redistributed by the practice, and whether the practitioner is running a real independent business, with their own ABN, their own invoicing, the ability to bring in someone else to do the work. None of those are decisive on their own, which is exactly why this is worth reviewing properly rather than guessing, ideally before a revenue office review does the reviewing for you.
