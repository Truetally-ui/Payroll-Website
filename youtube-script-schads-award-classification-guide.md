If you run payroll for an NDIS support provider, here's an uncomfortable fact. The Fair Work Ombudsman consistently finds that the single biggest cause of underpayment in this sector isn't a missed sleepover allowance or a penalty rate mistake, it's getting the classification wrong in the first place. And because classification sits underneath every other pay calculation, one wrong decision made on day one of someone's employment can quietly compound for years. So today I want to walk through how SCHADS classification actually works, where providers get it wrong, and a major change coming to the whole structure.

[SECTION 1 - WHAT SCHADS ACTUALLY COVERS]
The SCHADS Award, officially the Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award 2010, sets pay rates and classification levels across several parts of the sector. For most NDIS support providers, the schedule that matters is Schedule B, Social and Community Services employees, sometimes shortened to SACS. That's the schedule that covers most disability support workers delivering NDIS supports, and it's built around eight classification levels.

[SECTION 2 - THE TEST IS DUTIES, NOT TITLES]
Here's the part that trips a lot of providers up. Classification isn't decided by a job title or by what the employment contract calls the role. It's decided by what the person actually does, their level of autonomy, how complex the support is, and their qualifications or equivalent experience, matched against the descriptors written into Schedule B. So if someone with the title "support worker" is actually doing medication assistance or implementing a behaviour support plan with limited supervision, that's Level 3 work, and they need to be paid at Level 3, regardless of what their letter of offer says.

[SECTION 3 - THE HOME CARE TRAP]
There's also a structural trap worth knowing about. The SCHADS Award doesn't use one classification system for the whole sector. Home care employees, who mostly do personal care and domestic assistance under aged-care style arrangements, sit under a separate four-level structure. Disability support workers delivering NDIS supports should be classified under the eight-level Schedule B structure instead. Providers who default new disability support hires into the home care classification, sometimes just because that's what the payroll system defaults to, can end up underpaying for work that should sit under SACS. Checking which schedule applies at all, before you even get to picking a level, is step one.

[SECTION 4 - WHY THIS COMPOUNDS]
Classification isn't a standalone number. Base rate, penalty rates, sleepover and broken shift allowances, and overtime are all calculated on top of it. Get the level wrong when someone's hired, and every one of those calculations is wrong from that point forward, every single pay cycle, until someone actually reviews it. A worker sitting at Level 2 while genuinely doing Level 3 work for two years isn't a small error, it's two years of underpayment built into every pay item that touches that base rate. That compounding effect is exactly why classification, not allowances, is what the Fair Work Ombudsman keeps flagging as the real driver of underpayment findings.

[SECTION 5 - THE 2026 RESTRUCTURE]
And there's a bigger change coming. On the first of June 2026, the Fair Work Commission's Full Bench handed down its decision in the gender-based undervaluation review of the SCHADS Award, finding the existing classification structure had undervalued work across the sector. That decision introduces a brand new integrated classification structure, replacing the current Schedules B, C, E and F. It phases in from the first of October 2026 for home care disability employees, and from the first of October 2027 for the rest, including Schedule B. No one's minimum rate goes backwards in the transition, but every existing classification will still need to be reviewed and correctly translated when it lands. Which makes right now, well before either deadline, the time to confirm your current classifications are actually correct, rather than carrying an existing mistake straight into the new structure.
