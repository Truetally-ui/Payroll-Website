[HOOK]
Allied health payroll looks simple from the outside. Staff get rostered, sessions get delivered, wages go out. But it's actually one of the more complex payroll environments to run properly, because a big chunk of practitioners in most clinics are paid as contractors, not employees, and the income behind those pay runs comes in through several different billing channels on completely different timeframes. So today I want to walk through what allied health payroll services actually need to get right, not just process.

[SECTION 1 - AWARD CLASSIFICATION]
Start with your employed staff. Clinical and admin roles in most allied health settings sit under the Health Professionals and Support Services Award, or sometimes the Clerks Award for reception and admin depending on how the clinic structures classifications. Getting the classification level right isn't a one-time job at hiring, it needs to be reviewed as duties and qualifications change, because it drives minimum rates, penalty rates for early, late and weekend appointments, and allowances. A classification that was correct three years ago isn't automatically still correct today.

[SECTION 2 - CONTRACTOR VERSUS EMPLOYEE]
Here's the bigger issue though. A lot of allied health practitioners work under a service agreement, paying the clinic a percentage of billings or a room fee for the space and admin support. Whether that's genuinely a contractor relationship comes down to the substance of the arrangement, not just what the contract says, and since the High Court's decisions in Personnel Contracting and Jamsek back in 2022, the written contract terms are the primary focus. But things like who controls the clinic hours, who owns the equipment and patient records, and whether the practitioner can subcontract the work, still matter to how the ATO and state revenue offices look at this for superannuation and payroll tax specifically.

[SECTION 3 - PAYROLL TAX RISK]
And that leads into something a lot of allied health clinics haven't looked at closely enough. Since the Thomas and Naaz line of cases involving medical centres, state revenue offices have been applying the relevant contract provisions in payroll tax law to practices paying practitioners under service agreements. Most of the published guidance so far has talked about medical centres specifically, but the underlying test in payroll tax legislation isn't written to be medical-only, and several states have issued broader guidance touching health service arrangements generally. If your clinic runs a similar percentage-of-billings model, this is worth checking against current guidance for your specific state, not assuming it's a GP-only issue.

[SECTION 4 - SUPERANNUATION, THE PART EVERYONE MISSES]
Now, superannuation. For employees it's straightforward, twelve percent of ordinary time earnings, the rate that's been in place since the first of July 2025. But here's the part that trips up a lot of clinics, a practitioner can be correctly treated as a contractor for tax and payroll tax purposes and still be owed superannuation guarantee, because the Superannuation Guarantee Act has its own extended definition that covers anyone paid wholly or principally for their labour. So contractor status for one purpose doesn't automatically mean contractor status for super. That's a separate check, and it's one of the most commonly missed items in allied health payroll.

[SECTION 5 - BILLING CYCLES AND MULTI-SITE REPORTING]
Then there's the reconciliation problem. Allied health income comes through Medicare, private health funds, NDIS plan managers, and direct client payment, all on different processing timeframes. If your pay run is built purely around a calendar cycle without reconciling actual sessions against what's been billed, that's how a cancelled appointment gets paid anyway, or a completed session never makes it into the pay calculation at all. And if you're running more than one clinic location, Single Touch Payroll Phase 2 needs each employee's income type and pay location coded correctly, especially if staff move between sites or get paid centrally.
