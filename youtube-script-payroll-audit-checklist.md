## Script (~850 words)

[HOOK]
Most payroll problems don't show up as one big obvious mistake. They build up quietly, a classification that never got updated when someone's role changed, a super rate that's a percentage point out of date, a contractor arrangement that's slowly drifted into something that actually looks like employment. So today I want to walk through a practical payroll audit checklist, six areas you can check yourself, right now, before any of that quietly becomes an expensive problem.

[SECTION 1 - AWARD CLASSIFICATION AND PAY RATES]
Start here, because misclassification is the single most common source of underpayment, and it's almost never deliberate. Check that every employee is mapped to the award or agreement that actually applies to the work they do right now, not the role they were hired into. If you're using something like the SCHADS Award, check the classification level reflects actual duties and qualifications, not just a job title. And check your base rates, loadings and allowances have actually been updated for the most recent annual wage review, because award rates change every year and payroll systems don't update themselves.

[SECTION 2 - RECORD KEEPING AND PAYSLIPS]
Next, records. Under the Fair Work Regulations 2009, you need to keep time and wages records, and the Fair Work Ombudsman expects most employee records kept for a minimum of seven years, including for people who've left. Check your payslips specifically, they need to go out within one working day of payment, and include the ABN, the pay period, gross and net pay, the super contribution amount, and the actual pay rate applied. This sounds basic, but it's one of the most commonly cited issues in Fair Work Ombudsman compliance activity.

[SECTION 3 - SUPERANNUATION GUARANTEE]
Super hit its final scheduled increase, 12 percent, on the first of July 2025, and it applies to ordinary time earnings paid on or after that date. Check that 12 percent is actually what's calculating, check contributions are going out by the quarterly due date at minimum, and check your ordinary time earnings mapping correctly includes the allowances that count and excludes the ones that don't. This one matters because if you get it wrong, it's not just a back payment, it's the Superannuation Guarantee Charge, which includes interest, an administration fee, and isn't tax deductible.

[SECTION 4 - STP AND ATO REPORTING]
Here's a distinction worth making, a pay run can be calculated correctly and still be reported incorrectly. So check pay events are actually lodging on or before payday, check income types and cessation reasons are coded properly, and check your STP finalisation declaration actually went in by the 14th of July deadline for every completed financial year. This is a separate check from the pay calculation itself, don't assume one being right means the other is too.

[SECTION 5 - LEAVE, AND CONTRACTOR VS EMPLOYEE]
Two more things worth a look. First, leave accrual, this tends to only surface as a problem when someone takes leave or leaves the business, so check the accrual base and loading are correct now, before that happens. Second, and this one sits outside the payroll system entirely, anyone you're paying as a contractor. The test isn't what the contract says, it's the actual relationship, set hours, using the business's equipment, unable to subcontract, economically dependent on one engagement. Any of those point toward employment regardless of the label, and for medical practices specifically, this exact issue has been the subject of active state revenue office payroll tax rulings.

[SECTION 6 - WHY THIS MATTERS MORE NOW]
And here's why running this checklist regularly actually matters more than it used to. Since the first of January 2025, the Fair Work Act has included a criminal offence for intentional wage theft, penalties up to 8.25 million dollars for a company, and for an individual, fines up to 1.65 million dollars or up to ten years in prison. Intent is required, so a genuine mistake you catch and fix doesn't meet that threshold. But here's the thing, having a documented, regularly repeated audit process is one of the clearest ways to actually demonstrate an error was accidental rather than something you ignored. So this isn't just a compliance nicety anymore, it's part of how you manage that risk.
