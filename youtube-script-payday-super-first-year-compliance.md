[HOOK]
Payday Super has been live since 1 July, and if you've had even one super payment slip past the seven-day window since then, you've probably wondered what actually happens next. The ATO answered that question back in January, with a guideline called PCG 2026/1, and most employers still haven't read it. It splits every employer into a low, medium or high risk zone, and which zone you land in depends less on whether you make a mistake and more on what you do in the days after. Let's go through exactly how it works.

[SECTION 1 - WHAT PCG 2026/1 ACTUALLY IS]
PCG 2026/1 is what's called a Practical Compliance Guideline, finalised by the ATO on 28 January 2026. It covers the first year Payday Super operates, so any superannuation guarantee shortfall tied to a qualifying earnings day between 1 July 2026 and 30 June 2027. Here's the important bit: this guideline doesn't change the law. The seven-business-day payment requirement still applies from 1 July 2026, full stop. What PCG 2026/1 does is tell you how the ATO is going to prioritise its compliance resources during that first year, based on how you behave once something goes wrong.

[SECTION 2 - THE THREE RISK ZONES]
There are three zones, and they're worth knowing cold. Low risk is where you genuinely intended to pay on time, the contribution just didn't land in the fund on time for reasons outside your control, say, a clearing house delay, and you fix it as soon as reasonably practicable. The ATO says flat out it won't have cause to review your actions if you're in that zone. Medium risk is where you don't quite meet that bar, but you still get every employee's individual shortfall down to nil within 28 days of the end of the quarter the earnings were paid in. Think of an employer still running super on something closer to a quarterly rhythm instead of the new seven-day cycle, but who ultimately squares everything up. That gets lower priority than high risk, but it's not automatically waved through. High risk is the one to avoid: any employee shortfall still sitting above nil after that 28-day window. That's the ATO's stated compliance priority for the whole transition year.

[SECTION 3 - WHAT COUNTS AS A QUALIFYING EARNINGS DAY]
Quick definition, because this term comes up constantly. A qualifying earnings day, or QE day, is the payday that actually triggers your super guarantee obligation under the new system. The payment types that count are basically the same ones that already counted before 1 July, things like ordinary time earnings. Once that QE day happens, the clock starts, and the contribution needs to reach the employee's fund within seven business days. The three risk zones sit downstream of that clock. Missing the seven days doesn't automatically put you in the high risk zone, what actually matters is how fast you catch it and fix it afterwards.

[SECTION 4 - WHY THIS HITS NDIS, MEDICAL AND ALLIED HEALTH HARDER]
This is the part I really want NDIS providers, medical practices and allied health clinics to sit with. This framework applies to every employer, but it's genuinely harder to stay in the low risk zone when you're running a big casual support worker roster under the SCHADS Award, or a mix of employed and contracted practitioners, or clinicians spread across multiple pay cycles. Every extra pay cycle, every roster import, every timesheet feed is another place a qualifying earnings figure can go slightly wrong, or a payment can miss the window. And if you're engaging any of your workforce through a labour hire arrangement or a service entity, there's a whole separate question about who's actually the employer for super guarantee purposes in the first place, which the ATO's dealing with in a related draft ruling. More moving parts means more chances for a shortfall, which means the speed of your error-correction process matters more, not less.

[SECTION 5 - HOW TO STAY IN THE LOW RISK ZONE]
Five things I'd actually do. One, double check every pay item feeding into qualifying earnings is mapped correctly, so the obligation calculated each payday is right from the start. Two, don't just check that a super payment was submitted, confirm the fund actually received it inside the seven business days. Three, build an actual escalation process for a missed or short contribution, something that fires the moment it's caught, not something that waits for the next routine reconciliation. Four, keep a running list of any outstanding shortfall and make sure it hits zero well before that 28-day post-quarter deadline. And five, if you're using intermediaries or contractors anywhere in your workforce, sort out who's legally on the hook for the super obligation before a shortfall happens, not while you're scrambling to fix one.
