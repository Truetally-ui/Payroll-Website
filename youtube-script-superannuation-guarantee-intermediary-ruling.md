## Script (~850 words)

[HOOK]
If your business uses locums, agency staff, or contractors supplied through a service entity, here's a question worth asking before the ATO asks it for you: who is actually responsible for their superannuation? Not who pays the invoice, who is legally on the hook if the super hasn't been paid. The ATO just released a draft ruling that goes directly at this question, and if you run a medical practice, an allied health clinic or an NDIS service, there's a good chance it applies to at least one of your current staffing arrangements.

[SECTION 1 - THE RULING THAT JUST LANDED]
On 17 June 2026, the ATO released draft ruling SGR 2026/D1, Superannuation Guarantee, work arranged by intermediaries. It's specifically about three-way arrangements: an end-user business that needs the work done, an intermediary that supplies the worker, think labour hire firms, recruitment agencies, or service entities, and the worker themselves. Once it's finalised, it replaces the ATO's existing guidance on this exact question, which has been in place since 2005. The consultation period closed at the end of July 2026, so this is very much live guidance right now, not something sitting on a shelf.

[SECTION 2 - WHY THIS ISN'T AS OBVIOUS AS IT SOUNDS]
Here's the problem the ruling is trying to fix. When you employ someone directly, working out who owes their super is easy, it's you. But add a third party into the mix, and it stops being obvious. A medical centre books a locum GP through a locum agency. An allied health clinic contracts a physio through a service entity that also does the clinic's billing. An NDIS provider fills a roster gap through a staffing agency. In every one of those setups, more than one business could plausibly be described as involved in employing that worker, and only one of them is actually liable for the super if it turns out to be owed.

[SECTION 3 - THE TEST THE ATO ACTUALLY USES]
So how do you work out which one? The ruling says you start by identifying whether a contract for the performance of work exists, and between which parties, then you ask whether an employment relationship exists under section 12 of the Superannuation Guarantee Administration Act. That includes the ordinary meaning of employee, and an extended meaning under section 12(3), which deems someone an employee if they're working under a contract that's wholly or principally for their labour. This isn't brand new law, it was tested in the Dental Corporation versus Moffet case back in 2020, where the Full Federal Court found a dentist working through a service entity was still an employee for super purposes, because that's what the arrangement actually was underneath the paperwork. What SGR 2026/D1 adds is a clear statement that in these three-way arrangements, it's ultimate legal control over the worker under the contract that matters, not which business happens to direct their day-to-day tasks.

[SECTION 4 - WHY THIS HITS OUR THREE INDUSTRIES HARDER]
Medical, allied health and NDIS all lean on intermediary staffing more than most industries, which is exactly why this matters here specifically. Practice trusts and service entities are extremely common in medical and allied health. Labour hire and staffing agencies are common across NDIS rosters. None of these arrangements are automatically a problem, but none of them automatically mean the super obligation is handled either, and a lot of businesses have been assuming it's covered somewhere in the chain without actually checking.

[SECTION 5 - WHY THE TIMING MATTERS EVEN MORE NOW]
Here's what makes this urgent rather than theoretical. From 1 July 2026, Payday Super means superannuation guarantee has to be paid within 7 business days of every single payday, not once a quarter. If you've got the wrong party identified as the employer in one of these three-way arrangements, that mistake used to have a full quarter to get caught before a payment was actually missed. Under Payday Super, the same mistake produces a missed payment on every single pay run, weekly or fortnightly for most of these arrangements. The cost of getting this analysis wrong just went up by roughly ten times in frequency.

[SECTION 6 - WHAT TO ACTUALLY DO ABOUT IT]
Start by listing every locum, agency, labour hire or service entity arrangement you currently use, not just your direct employees. For each one, work out which contract is actually for the worker's labour, and who holds legal control under that contract, not who manages them day to day. Don't rely on an ABN or a contractor label as proof the question's been answered, because it isn't. And once SGR 2026/D1 is finalised, get your locum and service entity agreements reviewed against it properly, because it's going to become the ATO's primary word on this question for the next couple of decades, the same way its 2005 predecessor was.
