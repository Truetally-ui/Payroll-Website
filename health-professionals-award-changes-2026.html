Health Professionals Award Changes 2026: What Allied Health Employers Must Do Before 1 October

Quick answer

The Fair Work Commission's decision [2026] FWCFB 123, handed down 26 May 2026 in case AM2024/20, rewrites the classification structure and increases minimum pay rates across the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2020 (MA000027). The new classifications and the first stage of increased rates apply from the first full pay period starting on or after 1 October 2026, with four further increases phased in yearly to 2030. Allied health employers covered by this award need to reclassify every affected employee and update their payroll systems before that first pay period, not after it.

If your clinic employs physiotherapists, occupational therapists, psychologists, speech pathologists, podiatrists, dietitians or audiologists under the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2020, a decision handed down by the Fair Work Commission in May 2026 changes how you classify and pay them from 1 October 2026. This is a separate award and a separate decision from the SCHADS Award changes affecting disability support workers, and it deserves its own compliance checklist.

What the Fair Work Commission Decided

On 26 May 2026, a Fair Work Commission Expert Panel handed down decision [2026] FWCFB 123 in case AM2024/20. The decision finalises variations to the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2020 (MA000027) as part of the Commission's gender-based undervaluation priority awards review, the same broader review that has already produced changes to the Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award. The Health Professionals and Support Services Award covers allied health professionals employed in private practice settings, which makes this decision directly relevant to most allied health clinics operating outside the public hospital system.

The decision does two things at once: it replaces the award's existing classification structure with a new one, and it lifts minimum pay rates for most classifications under that new structure. Both changes commence from the first full pay period starting on or after 1 October 2026.

Why Pay Rates Are Rising: Gender-Based Undervaluation

The Expert Panel's finding underpinning this decision is that minimum rates in the Health Professionals and Support Services Award have been suppressed for years because the workforce is historically female-dominated. In plain English, that means work requiring a university qualification and ongoing professional responsibility, done by a workforce that skews heavily female, has historically been paid less than comparable qualifications and responsibilities in occupations that skew male. The Commission calls this gender-based undervaluation, and it is the same legal and factual basis used across the broader priority awards review, which has also examined aged care, early childhood education and the disability support sector.

This matters for how employers should read the decision. It is not a routine annual wage adjustment that tracks inflation. It is a structural correction the Commission has found was overdue, which is also why the increase is being phased in over five years rather than applied in a single step.

The New Classification Structure

The most significant structural change is how employees progress through classification levels. Under the previous structure, part-time and casual employees needed to accumulate a set number of hours, 1,824 under the old clause 17.1, before progressing to a higher classification. Under the new structure introduced by [2026] FWCFB 123, that hours-accumulation rule is removed. Progression through Level 1 is instead based on an employee's qualifications and years of experience in the profession, regardless of whether they work full time, part time or casually and regardless of how many hours they have logged.

Practically, this means an employer cannot rely on an employee's rostered hours to work out where they sit on the new scale. Two employees with the same years of experience in the same profession progress at the same rate, whether one works three days a week and the other works five. Every existing classification needs to be re-checked against qualifications and years of experience in the profession, not against a running hours tally carried over from the old system.

How Much Rates Are Rising, and When

Increases vary by classification and experience, but the general pattern the Commission has set out is a roughly 30% increase to minimum rates for graduate-level employees in professions requiring a university degree, and a roughly 20% to 25% increase for experienced practitioners, once all five stages are complete. The first of those five stages applies from the first full pay period on or after 1 October 2026. The remaining four stages are spaced roughly a year apart, operating from 30 June 2027, 30 June 2028, 30 June 2029 and 30 June 2030.

The rate that applies from 1 October 2026 is only the first instalment of the total increase, not the full amount. Budgeting for the 2026 stage alone, and assuming the rest is years away, is how practices end up under-provisioned for the increases that follow on the same schedule every year after.

Which Allied Health Professions Are Covered

The Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2020 covers a broad range of allied health professions working in private practice, including physiotherapy, occupational therapy, psychology, speech pathology, podiatry, dietetics, audiology, and social work, among others. Coverage depends on the specific classification definitions in the award and the nature of the employment relationship, so a practice employing a mix of these professions, or engaging some practitioners as contractors rather than employees, should confirm coverage classification by classification rather than assuming the whole workforce sits on the same footing.

This decision sits alongside the separate 2026 changes to the Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award, which affect a different workforce, disability support workers under the SCHADS Award rather than allied health professionals under MA000027. A clinic or provider that employs both allied health practitioners and disability support staff needs to track both sets of changes separately, since they run on different classification structures and different timelines. What Happens If You Get It Wrong

Paying a health professional below the correct minimum rate under the new classification structure is an underpayment, and the remedy is back pay of the shortfall for every affected pay period, plus the superannuation guarantee shortfall that arises on that back pay once it's calculated. The Fair Work Ombudsman has audit and enforcement powers over award compliance, and a systemic misclassification across a whole practice, rather than a single payroll error, is the kind of issue that draws closer scrutiny once it's identified. Because the increase is staged over five years, a classification error made now under the new structure doesn't just affect the 1 October 2026 rate, it compounds through every later stage until it's corrected.

Frequently Asked Questions

What changed in the Health Professionals and Support Services Award in 2026?

On 26 May 2026, a Fair Work Commission Expert Panel handed down decision [2026] FWCFB 123 in case AM2024/20, as part of the Commission's gender-based undervaluation priority awards review. The decision rewrites the classification structure and increases minimum pay rates for most employees covered by the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2020 (MA000027), which covers allied health professionals including physiotherapists, occupational therapists, psychologists, speech pathologists, podiatrists, dietitians and audiologists working in private practice.

When do the new Health Professionals Award pay rates start?

The new classification structure and the first stage of increased minimum rates apply from the first full pay period starting on or after 1 October 2026. There is no grace period. The remaining increases phase in over four further stages, each roughly a year apart, operating from 30 June 2027, 30 June 2028, 30 June 2029 and 30 June 2030.

Why are health professionals getting a pay rise under this decision?

The Fair Work Commission's Expert Panel found that minimum rates in the Health Professionals and Support Services Award had been suppressed because the workforce is historically female-dominated, a finding it calls gender-based undervaluation. The review compared the award's rates against comparable qualifications and responsibilities in less female-dominated occupations and found the gap could not be explained by anything other than the gendered composition of the workforce.

How much are pay rates increasing under the new classification structure?

Increases vary by classification level and years of experience. Graduate employees in professions requiring a university degree are looking at increases of roughly 30% to their minimum award rate once all five stages are complete, while experienced practitioners are generally looking at increases in the order of 20% to 25%. Because the increase is staged over five steps to 2030, the amount that applies from 1 October 2026 is only the first portion of that total increase, not the full amount.

What do allied health employers need to do before 1 October 2026?

Reclassify every award-covered employee under the new structure, recalculate minimum base rates for the first full pay period starting on or after 1 October 2026, update payroll software and Single Touch Payroll settings to reflect the new classification codes and rates, and check that any above-award or annualised salary arrangements still clear the new minimum once the increase applies. Employers should also review rosters and cash flow forecasts, since the increase compounds through casual loading, overtime and superannuation guarantee calculations.
