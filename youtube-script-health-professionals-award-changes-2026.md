[SECTION 1 - THE DECISION THAT LANDED IN MAY]
On 26 May 2026, a Fair Work Commission Expert Panel handed down decision [2026] FWCFB 123, in a case numbered AM2024/20. It's part of the Commission's gender-based undervaluation priority awards review, the same broad review that's already reshaped the SCHADS Award for disability support workers. This particular decision applies to the Health Professionals and Support Services Award 2020, award code MA000027, which covers allied health professionals working in private practice. Two things changed at once: the classification structure got rebuilt, and minimum pay rates went up for most classifications under that new structure.

[SECTION 2 - WHY THE COMMISSION DID THIS]
Here's the reasoning behind it, and it matters because it explains why this isn't a normal annual wage adjustment. The Expert Panel found that minimum rates in this award have been suppressed for years, specifically because the workforce is historically female-dominated. In plain terms, work that needs a university degree and real ongoing professional responsibility, done by a workforce that skews heavily female, has been paid less than comparable roles in occupations that skew male. That's gender-based undervaluation, and it's the same legal basis the Commission has already applied to aged care, early childhood education and disability support. Because the Commission found this was a structural problem, not a cost-of-living catch-up, the fix is being phased in over five years instead of landing in one go.

[SECTION 3 - THE CLASSIFICATION STRUCTURE ITSELF CHANGED]
This is the part that trips employers up first. Under the old award, part-time and casual employees had to bank up 1,824 hours before they could progress to a higher classification. That hours-counting rule is gone. Under the new structure, progression through Level 1 is based on qualifications and years of experience in the profession, full stop, regardless of whether someone works five days a week or two. So if you've been tracking classification by rostered hours, that method doesn't work anymore. You need to go back through every employee and reclassify them against years of experience in the profession, not a running hours tally.

[SECTION 4 - THE MONEY, AND THE TIMELINE]
On the pay side, graduate employees in professions requiring a university degree are looking at roughly a 30% increase to their minimum rate once the whole thing is finished, and experienced practitioners are generally in the 20 to 25% range. But that full number doesn't land on 1 October. It's staged across five steps: the first from the first full pay period on or after 1 October 2026, and then four more, roughly a year apart, from 30 June 2027, 2028, 2029 and 2030. If you only budget for the October instalment and assume the rest is years off, you're going to be caught out every single year after this one, because each stage compounds on the last.

This award covers a wide spread of allied health professions in private practice, physiotherapy, occupational therapy, psychology, speech pathology, podiatry, dietetics, audiology, social work, and more. Coverage comes down to the specific classification definitions and how someone's actually employed, so if your clinic has a mix of employees and contractors, or a mix of professions, check coverage person by person rather than assuming everyone sits in the same boat.
