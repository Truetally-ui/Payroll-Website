How to Classify Support Workers Under the SCHADS Award

Quick answer

Correct SCHADS classification means matching a support worker's actual duties, autonomy, and qualifications each fortnight against the 8 levels in Schedule B of the Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award 2010, not their job title or contract wording. Classification errors, not allowance or penalty rate mistakes, are the Fair Work Ombudsman's most cited driver of underpayment in the disability sector, and a new Fair Work Commission classification structure starts replacing Schedule B from 1 October 2027, so an outdated classification review is now a two part problem, not one.

Getting a support worker's classification wrong under the SCHADS Award is rarely a one-off mistake. It's usually set once at hiring and then carried forward, unreviewed, for years, which is exactly why it's the single most common source of underpayment claims we see in NDIS payroll. Here's how Schedule B classification actually works, where providers get it wrong, and what's about to change.

What the SCHADS Award Covers

The Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award 2010 (MA000100), known as the SCHADS Award, sets minimum pay rates, classification levels, allowances, and penalty rates for most employees in the social and community services, home care, disability, and crisis accommodation sectors in Australia. For NDIS support providers specifically, the classification schedule that applies to most support workers delivering disability supports is Schedule B, Social and Community Services (SACS) employees. Getting the right schedule and the right level within it is the foundation every other payroll calculation is built on.

The Classification Test: Duties, Not Titles

The Fair Work Ombudsman is clear that classification is decided by the work actually performed, not by a job title, a position description written years ago, or what the employment contract happens to call the role. The correct approach is to read the classification descriptors in Schedule B against what the support worker genuinely does in a typical fortnight, the complexity of the support provided, how much supervision they work under, and the qualifications or equivalent experience they bring to the role. A worker with the title "support worker" who is actually performing complex behaviour support or medication assistance with limited supervision needs to be paid at the level that reflects that work, regardless of what the letter of offer says.

Schedule B Levels 1 to 8 at a Glance

LevelGeneral character of the role
Level 1Entry or induction period, or basic ancillary and administrative tasks performed under direct supervision, no formal qualification required
Level 2Direct support and community access work performed with some independence, typically Certificate III or equivalent experience
Level 3More complex or specialised support with limited supervision, such as medication assistance or implementing a behaviour support plan, typically Certificate IV or equivalent experience
Level 4Substantial autonomy, may include coordinating the work of Level 1 to 3 employees or a specific program area
Level 5Coordinates a work unit or program, or provides a specialist service, generally requiring a relevant degree or equivalent professional experience
Level 6 to 8Senior management, supervision of other qualified staff, or highly specialised practitioner roles, requiring degree-level qualifications and significant experience

These descriptions are general orientation only. The full wording of every level and pay point sits in Schedule B of the Award itself and the Fair Work Ombudsman's published pay guide for MA000100, and it's that wording, not a summary table, that should be checked against actual duties before a classification decision is finalised or changed.

Home Care vs SACS: A Common Misclassification Trap

The SCHADS Award doesn't use one classification structure for the whole sector. Home care employees, who typically provide personal care and domestic assistance under aged care style arrangements, are classified under a separate 4-level home care structure, while Social and Community Services employees, the schedule that covers most NDIS disability support workers, sit under the 8-level Schedule B structure described above. Providers who bring on disability support staff and classify them under the home care schedule, either by habit or because a payroll system defaults to it, can end up paying materially less than the correct SACS classification for broadly similar work. Confirming which schedule actually applies to an NDIS support role, before assigning a level within it, is the first check, not an afterthought.

Why Classification Errors Drive Underpayment

Classification sits underneath every other payroll calculation. Base rate, penalty rates for weekend, evening, and public holiday work, sleepover and broken shift allowances, and overtime are all calculated as a percentage or fixed amount on top of the classification level's base rate. Set the classification wrong at hiring and every one of those downstream calculations is wrong too, compounding every pay cycle until someone reviews it. A support worker classified at Level 2 while genuinely performing Level 3 duties for two years generates two years of underpayment exposure across every pay item built on that base rate, which is why the Fair Work Ombudsman consistently names classification, not allowance or penalty rate errors, as the primary driver of underpayment findings in the disability and community services sector.

The 2026 Fair Work Commission Restructure

On 1 June 2026, the Fair Work Commission's Full Bench handed down its decision in the gender-based undervaluation review of the SCHADS Award, [2026] FWCFB 137, finding that the existing classification structure and wage rates had been affected by longstanding gender-based undervaluation of work in the sector. The decision introduces a new integrated classification structure that replaces the current Schedules B, C, E and F, alongside a reset of minimum wage rates and removal of the existing Equal Remuneration Order. The new arrangements start from 1 October 2026 for employees currently covered by Schedule E, Home Care Employees, Disability Care, and from 1 October 2027 for the remaining affected schedules, which includes Schedule B and most NDIS support workers. The Commission has confirmed no existing employee's minimum pay rate will be reduced as a result of the transition, and where a current rate already exceeds the translated rate under the new structure, the higher current rate is retained. Every existing classification decision will still need to be reviewed and correctly translated when the new structure takes effect, which makes now, well before the transition date, the right time to confirm current classifications are accurate rather than carrying an existing error into the new structure.

Running a Classification Review

A useful classification review works from the duties up, not the payroll system down. That means listing what each support worker actually does across a representative roster period, matching that list against the current Schedule B descriptors (or the applicable home care classification, if genuinely relevant), checking any relevant qualification or years of equivalent experience, and documenting the reasoning behind the level assigned. This is worth doing at least annually and immediately whenever a worker's responsibilities change, such as taking on medication administration, a behaviour support plan, or supervision of other staff, rather than waiting for the position description to be rewritten or a complaint to be raised.

Getting SCHADS Classification Right in Payroll

Correct classification only holds up if the payroll system that runs off it is actually configured to reflect it, base rate, penalty rates, sleepover and broken shift allowances, and travel time all need to map correctly to the assigned level in Xero or whichever platform is in use, and that mapping needs to be checked whenever a classification changes or an award update takes effect. This is where a lot of otherwise correct classification decisions quietly break down, the level on paper is right, but the pay item configuration behind it hasn't kept pace.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you correctly classify a support worker under the SCHADS Award?

Classification is decided by matching what the support worker actually does in a typical fortnight, their level of autonomy, the complexity of the support they provide, and their qualifications or equivalent experience, against the descriptors in Schedule B of the Social, Community, Home Care and Disability Services Industry Award 2010, not by their job title or what the employment contract calls the role. The Fair Work Ombudsman treats the duties actually performed as the deciding factor, so a title of "support worker" covering Level 3 duties still needs to be paid at Level 3.

How many classification levels are there under Schedule B of the SCHADS Award?

Schedule B, which covers Social and Community Services (SACS) employees and most NDIS disability support workers, has 8 classification levels. Level 1 is an entry or induction level involving basic, closely supervised tasks with no care responsibilities. Levels increase through more complex, specialised, or higher-autonomy support work, up through senior practitioner, coordination, and management roles at the top levels, each generally requiring a higher qualification or a longer period of equivalent experience.

What's the difference between Home Care and Social and Community Services classifications?

The SCHADS Award contains separate classification schedules for different parts of the sector. Home care employees, who typically provide personal care and domestic assistance under aged care style arrangements, are classified under a 4-level structure, while Social and Community Services employees, which covers most disability support workers delivering NDIS supports, are classified under the 8-level Schedule B structure. Placing an NDIS disability support worker under the Home Care classification instead of the correct SACS classification is a recognised source of underpayment because the two streams carry different pay outcomes for broadly similar work.

Why are classification errors the most common cause of SCHADS underpayment?

Classification is usually set once, at the point a support worker is hired, and then left unreviewed as their duties, responsibilities, and qualifications change over time. Because every other payroll calculation, base rate, penalty rates, allowances, and overtime, is built on top of the classification level, an error made at hiring compounds every single pay cycle until someone catches it, which is why the Fair Work Ombudsman consistently identifies incorrect classification, rather than allowance or penalty rate mistakes, as the primary driver of underpayment in the disability and community services sector.

Is the SCHADS Award classification structure changing?

Yes. On 1 June 2026, the Fair Work Commission's Full Bench handed down its decision in the gender-based undervaluation review of the SCHADS Award, in case number [2026] FWCFB 137, finding that the existing classification structure undervalued work in the sector. The decision introduces a new integrated classification structure that replaces Schedules B, C, E and F, taking effect from 1 October 2026 for employees currently under Schedule E, Home Care Employees, Disability Care, and from 1 October 2027 for the remaining affected schedules including Schedule B. No employee's minimum pay rate will fall as a result of the transition.

How often should support worker classifications be reviewed?

Classifications should be reviewed on a set schedule, at least annually and whenever a support worker's duties change meaningfully, such as taking on medication administration, complex behaviour support, or supervising other staff, rather than only when a complaint, audit, or Fair Work Ombudsman inquiry forces the question. A scheduled review also needs to account for the SCHADS Award restructure taking effect from 1 October 2026 and 1 October 2027, since every existing classification will need to be translated to the new structure.
