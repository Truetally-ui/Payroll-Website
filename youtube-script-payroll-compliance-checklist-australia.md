If someone asked you right now whether your payroll was fully compliant, could you actually answer that with confidence, or would you need to go check a few things first? For most businesses we work with, it's the second one. Payroll compliance in Australia isn't one rule, it's several running at the same time, award rates, superannuation, reporting, record keeping, and it's really common for one of those to quietly fall out of date while everything else looks fine. So today I want to walk through the actual checklist we use when we review a payroll file, and why each item on it matters.

[SECTION 1 — WHY PAYROLL COMPLIANCE ISN'T ONE THING]
The first thing worth understanding is that payroll compliance in Australia spans at least three separate regulatory relationships. Award pay rates and the National Employment Standards sit under the Fair Work Act, enforced by the Fair Work Ombudsman. Superannuation guarantee obligations sit under a completely different piece of legislation, the Superannuation Guarantee Administration Act, and it's the ATO that enforces that one. And pay event reporting runs through Single Touch Payroll. A business can be doing everything right on one of these fronts and still be exposed on another, which is exactly why a single "payroll's fine" assumption doesn't hold up well to a proper review. And the Fair Work Ombudsman's own enforcement numbers back this up, they recorded a record 23.7 million dollars in court penalties in the 2024-25 financial year alone.

[SECTION 2 — AWARD CLASSIFICATION AND PAY RATES]
The single biggest source of underpayment we see is incorrect award classification. Every other calculation, base rate, penalty rates, overtime, allowances, leave loading, flows from that one classification decision, so if it's wrong at the start, everything downstream is wrong too. This is a particular risk under the SCHADS Award, common across NDIS and allied health, because classification depends on the actual duties someone's doing, not their job title, and duties tend to drift over time without anyone going back to reassess the classification. On top of that, award rates are usually varied by the Fair Work Commission from the first full pay period on or after the first of July each year. A rate that was correct in June can quietly be underpaying by July if that update doesn't get applied, and that's one of the most common gaps we find, because it requires someone to actively make the change rather than your software just handling it.

[SECTION 3 — SUPERANNUATION AND PAYDAY SUPER]
Superannuation guarantee reached 12 percent from the first of July 2025 and it's staying there through this financial year. Get the ordinary time earnings calculation wrong, or miss a payment, and you trigger the ATO's superannuation guarantee charge, which isn't tax deductible and comes with interest and an admin fee stacked on top. The bigger structural shift is Payday Super, starting from the first of July 2026, which moves super from a quarterly obligation to something due in line with every single pay run. That shrinks the window you've got to catch a misconfigured pay item before it compounds across dozens of pay cycles instead of just four a year.

[SECTION 4 — STP PHASE 2 AND RECORD KEEPING]
STP Phase 2 asks for a lot more detail with every pay event than the original version did, disaggregated pay components, an income type for every payment, a cessation reason whenever someone leaves. Because those reports go straight to the ATO, a mapping error shows up in your year-to-date figures immediately rather than staying hidden until some later lodgement. Separately from that, Fair Work Regulations require you to keep time and wages records for at least seven years, and to get a payslip into an employee's hands within one working day of paying them, with specific content requirements attached. Skipping either of these doesn't just risk a finding against you, it also makes it a lot harder to defend a pay decision later if it's ever challenged.

[SECTION 5 — WAGE THEFT AND WHY THIS MATTERS MORE NOW]
Here's the part that's changed the stakes considerably. Since the first of January 2025, intentionally underpaying an employee, whether that's wages, super, leave, overtime, penalty rates or allowances, is now a criminal offence under the Fair Work Act, not just a civil one. We're talking penalties of up to ten years imprisonment for an individual, and fines running into the millions for a company. An honest mistake in a pay category is still a civil underpayment issue and still needs fixing, but that criminal threshold specifically targets intentional conduct, which is exactly why having a documented, regularly reviewed process matters so much more than it used to.
