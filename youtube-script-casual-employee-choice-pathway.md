If you employ casual staff in Australia, here's a scenario worth thinking through before it happens to you. One of your longest-serving casual support workers hands you a written notice saying they believe they're not genuinely casual anymore, and they want to become permanent. You now have exactly 21 days to respond. This is the employee choice pathway, and if you run an NDIS service, a medical practice or an allied health clinic with a big casual roster, it's more likely to land on your desk than almost any other employer type.

[SECTION 1 - WHAT THE EMPLOYEE CHOICE PATHWAY ACTUALLY IS]
The employee choice pathway is the current process under the Fair Work Act for a casual employee to move to permanent employment. It replaced the old system, where the obligation sat with the employer to review casual staff after 12 months and offer conversion if they qualified. Now, the employee starts the process. They give written notice stating they believe they no longer meet the legal definition of a casual employee, and that shifts responsibility onto you to respond properly.

[SECTION 2 - WHEN THIS ACTUALLY STARTED]
This isn't brand new legislation, but the timing matters for a lot of smaller providers. The employee choice pathway started on 26 February 2025 for larger employers, and on 26 August 2025 for small businesses with fewer than 15 employees. A lot of NDIS providers, medical practices and allied health clinics sit right under that threshold, which means this has only been live for just over a year for many of you, right around the point where a casual employee's qualifying period would first be reached.

[SECTION 3 - WHO CAN ACTUALLY GIVE A NOTICE]
Not every casual employee can trigger this. They need at least 6 months of employment, or 12 months if you're a small business, and they need to genuinely believe they no longer meet the casual definition, based on things like a regular, predictable pattern of hours that's continued for a while. That's the test the Fair Work Commission looks at if it ends up disputed, the real substance and practical reality of the work, not just what the contract says on paper.

[SECTION 4 - THE 21 DAY CLOCK]
Once a valid notice lands, you've got 21 days to respond in writing, and before you respond, you're required to consult with the employee about what the change would actually involve. You then either accept it, setting out the new employment type, hours and start date, or refuse it on specific grounds. You can only refuse if the employee genuinely still meets the casual definition, accepting it would require substantial changes to how the business runs, or it would breach a required recruitment process. Anything outside those grounds, or missing the 21-day window, isn't a valid refusal, and it can end up in front of the Fair Work Commission.

[SECTION 5 - WHAT CHANGES IN PAYROLL]
Here's the part that actually affects your pay run. When a conversion is accepted, the casual loading stops, typically 25% under awards like SCHADS, and the employee moves to the standard full-time or part-time rate for their classification. They start accruing annual leave and personal leave from the conversion date, since casuals don't accrue paid leave. Notice and redundancy entitlements start accruing too. None of this is backdated, it applies from the agreed effective date, but your payroll system needs the employment type, leave accrual rules and pay rate all changed together, because if you only update the headline pay rate and forget the leave accrual settings, that employee's leave balance is wrong from the very next pay run.

[SECTION 6 - WHAT TO DO BEFORE A NOTICE EVEN ARRIVES]
The employers who handle this well aren't scrambling inside the 21-day window, they already know which casual staff are approaching or past the eligibility threshold. That means actually tracking regular casual rosters against the practical reality test, not just relying on the original employment contract. For NDIS providers specifically, this often means support workers who've settled into the same weekly shifts for a year or more, even though they were engaged as casual. For medical and allied health, it's often long-serving reception or admin staff on the same pattern week after week.
